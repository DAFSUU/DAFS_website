Case study: what start-ups do, where they are, and who funds them

A topic model of 81,285 European start-up descriptions, compared with the industry labels of a start-up database

Author

Janpieter van der Pol

Start-up databases sort companies into industries that were chosen in advance: health, fintech, energy. These lists are useful, but they describe markets, they change slowly, and they have no name for a new group of companies. Yet every start-up in the database also has a description of what it does. This case study reads those descriptions with a topic model and asks what the topics show that the industry labels do not. Once every start-up has a topic, the topic becomes a variable like any other: we can compare it with the existing classification, map it, and follow the money that flows into it.

NoteThe research question

What does a topic model of start-up descriptions show that the industry labels of a start-up database do not, and how are these topics spread across Europe and linked by cross-border investment?

Step 1: the data

Dealroom tracks start-ups and their investors. Our export covers European start-ups, with for each start-up a tagline, a longer description, its headquarters, Dealroom’s industries and sub-industries, and its funding rounds with the investors that took part.

Start-ups 81,285 with at least one funding round in 2015–2025 and a description
Text per start-up tagline + first 1,500 characters of the description (median 1,550 characters)
Existing classification 28 industries (93% of start-ups have one) and 77 sub-industries
Investments 254,867: one investor taking part in one funding round, with the investor’s country
Investors 54,875

The descriptions need some cleaning before anything else: HTML tags, web addresses, a recurring MISSING ALT TEXT left over from the website scrape, and sentences glued together (“…seen.To do this…”).

clean <- function(x) {
  x <- stri_replace_all_regex(x, "MISSING ALT TEXT|<[^>]+>|https?://\\S+", " ")
  x <- stri_replace_all_regex(x, "([a-z])\\.([A-Z])", "$1. $2")    # "seen.To" -> "seen. To"
  squish(x)
}
startups[, text := paste(clean(tagline), substr(clean(description), 1, 1500))]

Step 2: the topic model

We embed every description with a sentence transformer, and let BERTopic (via the Rhobots package) group start-ups that describe themselves alike.

library(Rhobots)
encoder <- load_hf_bert("sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2")
emb <- embed_texts(encoder, startups$text, max_length = 384, device = "mps")   # ~25 minutes for 81,285 texts

# try several settings before choosing one
sweep <- sweep_topics(prep_terms(startups$text), embeddings = emb, n_neighbors = c(15, 30),
                      min_pts = c(50, 100, 200), extra_stopwords = su_words, seed = 42)

fit <- fit_bertopic(docs = prep_terms(startups$text), embeddings = emb, stopwords = sw,
                    umap_n_neighbors = 30, hdbscan_min_pts = 100, seed = 42)
fit <- reduce_outliers(fit)

We use 30/100: 99 topics, narrow enough to show niches inside industries but few enough to read, with good separation. The start-ups that fit no topic at first are assigned to the nearest one.

The stopword list matters. Many Dealroom descriptions follow one template (“X is a pioneering startup operating in the … sector, focusing on innovative solutions…”). Without words such as startup, platform, solution, innovative, operating, sector in the stopword list, those words are at the top of every topic. City and country names are removed as well, so that topics group start-ups by what they do rather than by where they are.

Labelling the topics, and two lessons on prompting

A local language model (qwen3 through Ollama) reads each topic’s top terms and its ten most typical start-ups and returns a label, plus a broad theme that we use only to colour the charts.

Lesson 1: one question per call. In the first run we asked for the label, the theme and a third judgement in one call. The labels were good, but 40 of the 99 topics got the theme Security and defence, including renewable energy, fashion and education. Asking for the theme in a separate call, with every theme described and a one-sentence reason asked for before the answer, gave sensible themes.

schema <- list(type = "object", required = list("reason", "theme"),
  properties = list(reason = list(type = "string"),                     # first: reason before the answer
                    theme  = list(type = "string", enum = names(themes))))

Lesson 2: not every judgement can be delegated. We also asked the model what holds each topic together: a market, a technology, a business model, a customer group or a societal challenge. Even with a separate call and a reason first, the answers were inconsistent (crypto infrastructure as a customer group, fashion brands as a societal challenge). We dropped this variable and measured the same idea from the data instead (Step 3).

Some labels were corrected by hand after reading the topics. One topic turned out to be a group of descriptions written in Dutch and Nordic languages: a language cluster, not a business. It is kept in the model but left out of the comparisons.

Step 3: topics and industries see different things

Each start-up now has two labels: Dealroom’s industry and its topic. How much do they agree?

# normalised mutual information between two groupings of the same start-ups (0 = unrelated, 1 = identical)
nmi <- function(a, b) {
  t <- table(a, b) / length(a); pa <- rowSums(t); pb <- colSums(t); nz <- t > 0
  mi <- sum(t[nz] * log(t[nz] / outer(pa, pb)[nz]))
  h  <- function(p) -sum(p[p > 0] * log(p[p > 0]))
  2 * mi / (h(pa) + h(pb))
}
nmi(startups$main_industry, startups$topic)          # 0.42
nmi(startups$main_industry, sample(startups$topic))  # 0.005 when the topics are shuffled

The two overlap, but far from fully. In 30% of the topics, no industry holds a majority of the start-ups.

One industry, many topics. To see how much an industry label lumps together, we count over how many topics its start-ups are spread. The effective number of topics is exp(entropy) of the shares: the number of equally large topics that would give the same spread.

Step 4: where the topics are

To map a topic we use the location quotient (LQ): the topic’s share among a region’s start-ups divided by its share in Europe. LQ = 2 means the topic is twice as common in that region as in Europe as a whole. Start-ups are placed in NUTS2 regions.

region_topic <- startups[!is.na(nuts2), .N, by = .(region = nuts2, topic)]
region_topic[, n_region := sum(N), by = region]
p_topic <- startups[, .(p = .N / nrow(startups)), by = topic]
region_topic[p_topic, lq := (N / n_region) / i.p, on = "topic"]
region_topic[N >= 15][order(-lq)][, head(.SD, 3), by = topic]       # hotspots per topic

Industry maps are dominated by London, Paris and Berlin. The topics show the specialised hubs behind them:

Step 5: who invests where, per topic

An investment is one investor taking part in one funding round. Comparing the investor’s country with the start-up’s country gives its origin.

inv <- merge(investments, startups[, .(startup_id, topic, s_cc = iso2)], by = "startup_id")
inv[, origin := fcase(is.na(i_cc), "unknown", i_cc == s_cc, "domestic",
                      i_cc %in% europe, "other Europe", i_cc == "US", "United States",
                      default = "rest of world")]
inv[origin != "unknown", .N, by = .(topic, origin)][, share := N / sum(N), by = topic]

The answer

TipWhat do topics show that industries do not?
  • Topics and industries are different maps. They agree only moderately (NMI 0.42), and in 30% of the topics no industry has a majority. Large industries break up into many businesses: 13 topics for 80% of health start-ups, 27 for enterprise software.
  • Topics find groups that the classification has no name for: technologies such as IoT, XR, geospatial AI and industrial manufacturing are spread over 11 to 14 effective industries, and some topics have no matching sub-industry at all.
  • Topics reveal regional specialisations that industry maps hide: aquaculture in Western Norway, offshore wind in Aberdeen, crypto in Zug, immunotherapy in Basel, satellites in Toulouse.
  • Where the money comes from depends on the topic: within fintech the US share ranges from 8% to 31%; green, industrial and deep-tech topics are funded mostly at home.
  • Countries invest abroad in different topics: German investors in medical technology, Dutch in climate, Swedish in construction and heat pumps, Swiss in a mix that includes quantum computing and insurance.

The full analysis

The topic space, the industry–topic comparison, maps for every topic, country specialisation, the origin of investments, the country-by-country matrix per topic and investor profiles. Use the menus to switch topics, themes and countries, and hover for details. Open the analysis in its own window.

What topics as a datapoint make possible

  1. A second view of an existing classification. Comparing topics with industries shows where the classification lumps different businesses together and where it scatters one group of companies.
  2. Groups without a name. A topic model has no list decided in advance, so it can find technologies and niches that the database’s categories do not have.
  3. Geography at a finer grain. A topic per start-up can be counted per region, and finer categories show specialised hubs that broad industries hide.
  4. Linking to networks. Joined to the investors, topics turn into flows: who funds what, from where.

What this case teaches

  1. Clean and stop the template words. Database descriptions are written in a template; without a stopword list for it, every topic looks the same.
  2. Try several settings. The number of topics is a choice (here between 53 and 179), not a result. Report which one you used and why.
  3. Ask a language model one thing at a time, reason first. A combined question gave nonsense themes; separate questions fixed it.
  4. Measure what you can, instead of asking for it. Whether a topic cuts across markets was better measured from the industry mix than judged by a language model.
  5. Read the topics. One topic was a language, not a business. Only reading the top terms and typical start-ups shows such things.

Questions for discussion

  1. A start-up offering AI for insurance gets one topic: AI or insurance. Dealroom gives it both industries. What are the advantages of each approach, and how would soft topic shares (a probability for every topic) change the comparison?
  2. Descriptions are written to attract investors. Could the topic model be picking up fashions in how start-ups present themselves (everything is “AI-powered”) rather than what they do? How would you test this?
  3. Crypto start-ups get most of their money from abroad, green and deep-tech start-ups from home. Think of three explanations, and of data that would tell them apart.
  4. The location quotient is unstable for small regions. What minimum number of start-ups would you require, and how would you show the uncertainty on the map?
  5. Take one topic and the investor country matrix. Do investors fund start-ups in neighbouring countries, in countries with the same language, or in the large hubs? Which further data (distance, language, trade) could you join to test this?
