Case study: framing the green transition

From a research question to an answer, with 84,000 European Parliament speeches

Author

Janpieter van der Pol

The tutorial teaches the methods one by one: topic models, named entities, zero-shot classification, maps. This case study shows what happens when you put them to work on one research question, from the first decisions to a defensible answer. Pay attention to the steps that did not work at first: they are as much part of the analysis as the results.

NoteThe research question

How has the EU’s discussion of the green transition shifted between environmental protection, competitiveness and security?

A good question tells you what to measure. This one asks about a discussion (so we need texts), about the green transition (so we must decide which texts are about it), about three angles or frames (so we must recognise them in a text), and about a shift (so we need time, and enough of it).

Step 1: whose discussion, and when?

“The EU” does not speak with one voice. We take the European Parliament, where Commission proposals on climate and energy are debated in public by members from every member state and political family. The data are all plenary speeches by MEPs from July 2009 to September 2023 (the EU Debates corpus, with English versions of every speech), linked to each MEP’s country and national party through the Parliament’s open data portal.

Speeches 84,428, by MEPs, procedural items left out
Period three parliamentary terms, 14 July 2009 – 14 September 2023
Per speech date, debate, MEP, political group, member state, national party (on the day of the speech), English text

Choices to notice: the Parliament stands in for “the EU” (the Commission or the Council might talk differently), and fourteen years make it possible to see change rather than a snapshot.

Step 2: which speeches are about the green transition?

First a definition: the transition to a low-carbon economy, meaning climate policy and emission cuts, the energy transition, the Green Deal and emissions trading, decarbonising industry and transport, and the circular economy. Biodiversity and nature protection are left out on purpose: as subjects, they would raise the environmental frame by the choice of topic alone.

Then two criteria, both needed:

  1. The speech is close in meaning to one of five descriptions of the transition (cosine similarity of sentence embeddings, as in the zero-shot part of the tutorial).
  2. It uses at least two words of the transition’s vocabulary (climate, emissions, renewable, Green Deal, hydrogen, …): a transparent check.
anchors <- c(
  "climate change and cutting greenhouse gas emissions to reach climate neutrality",
  "the energy transition to renewable energy, wind and solar power and energy efficiency",
  "the European Green Deal, the Fit for 55 package and the emissions trading system",
  "decarbonising industry, transport and buildings with clean technologies, hydrogen and electric cars",
  "the circular economy, sustainable products and resource efficiency")
encoder <- load_hf_bert("sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2")
a <- embed_texts(encoder, anchors)
unit <- function(m) m / sqrt(rowSums(m^2))
green_sim <- apply(unit(embeddings) %*% t(unit(a)), 1, max)   # closest description
green <- green_sim >= 0.45 & green_terms >= 2

The threshold of 0.45 was chosen by reading the speeches just above candidate thresholds: at 0.35 they were about employment and animal cages, at 0.45 mostly about energy and climate. Result: 5,706 speeches on the green transition, 6.8% of all speeches.

Step 3: recognising the three frames

A frame is an angle on a subject. The same law on renewable energy can be defended because it protects the climate, because it creates jobs, or because it frees Europe from Russian gas. We measure frames per sentence (52,581 sentences), because one speech often combines several.

Frame The green transition as …
Environmental protection protection of the climate, nature and the planet; the climate crisis, science, future generations
Competitiveness an economic matter: industry, jobs, growth, investment, innovation, costs and burdens, global competition
Security a matter of security: energy supply, dependence on imports, strategic autonomy, raw materials, geopolitics

The first attempt did not work

The obvious idea was the zero-shot approach from the tutorial: write a few example sentences per frame and give each sentence the frame of the most similar example. Reading the results showed the problem. Sentences such as “Coastal windmills create artificial reefs with plenty of life” came out as security. Every sentence in these speeches is about energy or climate, so similarity picks up the subject, not the angle. No threshold fixes that.

What worked: a classifier trained on labelled sentences

  1. A local language model (qwen3, through Ollama) labelled 3,234 sentences with one frame or none, given the definitions above: 1,800 drawn at random, plus extra sentences close to examples of each frame so that the rare security frame had enough cases.
  2. A multinomial logistic regression on the sentence embeddings learned from these labels.
  3. Its accuracy was measured by 5-fold cross-validation, scored on the random sentences only (the extra sentences are not representative).
library(nnet)
X <- sentence_embeddings[labelled, ]            # 768 numbers per sentence
y <- factor(labels, levels = c("env", "comp", "sec", "none"))
model <- multinom(y ~ X, decay = 0.1, MaxNWts = 10000, maxit = 400)
# cross-validation: fit on 4/5 of the labels, predict the other 1/5, five times
Frame Precision Recall F1
Environmental protection 61% 56% 0.58
Competitiveness 63% 63% 0.63
Security 81% 55% 0.65
None 84% 87% 0.85

Not perfect, but the errors are informative: the model mostly confuses a frame with none, and almost never one frame with another. Security is found less often than it occurs (recall 55%) but is rarely wrong when found (precision 81%).

Step 4: checking the measure

A single measure is a claim; several that agree are evidence. Three checks:

Check What it shows
Word lists (no model involved): words per 1,000 for each frame correlate with the classifier per speech: r = 0.44 (environment), 0.57 (competitiveness), 0.68 (security); the same trends over time
A reader: the language model reads 240 whole speeches (16 a year) and rates each frame 0–2 rank correlation with the sentence measure 0.45–0.55; same dominant frame in 68% of speeches (κ = 0.47)
The corpus filter: the same reader judges whether each speech is really about the green transition only 61% are, mostly speeches near the threshold, on energy prices, budgets or pollution

The last check found a real weakness, so it changed the analysis (step 6).

Step 5: the shift

Share of framed sentences, by period:

Period Environmental protection Competitiveness Security
2009–14 (term 7) 35% 59% 6%
2014–19 (term 8) 41% 55% 4%
July 2019 – Feb 2022 (term 9, before the invasion) 47% 48% 4%
Feb 2022 – Sept 2023 (after the invasion) 36% 50% 14%

Two questions before you interpret this:

  • Is it chance? Bootstrap intervals (resampling speeches) are narrow for the periods: security after the invasion is 13–15%.
  • Is it a real shift, or did different MEPs speak? If, for example, more MEPs from Poland spoke about energy after 2022, the mix would change without anyone changing their mind. A regression of each speech’s frame share on the period, within the same political group and member state (fixed effects, standard errors clustered by MEP), gives +7.9 points for security after the invasion (SE 1.1), nearly the same as the raw difference. The shift is real.
# frame share of a speech ~ period, without and with group and country fixed effects
lm(y_sec ~ period, data = speeches)
lm(y_sec ~ period + group + country + translated, data = speeches)

Step 6: does the answer depend on the selection?

The reader check showed that the filter of step 2 lets in speeches on energy prices and supply, and those use more security language. So the main comparison was repeated with stricter selections:

Selection Security, before → after the invasion Environment, before → after
main (similarity ≥ 0.45, ≥ 2 terms) 4% → 14% 47% → 36%
strict (≥ 0.55, ≥ 3 terms) 4% → 8% 46% → 40%
strictest (≥ 0.60, ≥ 4 terms) 2% → 6% 47% → 43%

The direction holds in every selection; the size of the security turn depends on whether debates on energy prices and supply count as part of the green-transition debate. An honest answer therefore gives a range, not one number.

The answer

TipHow has the discussion shifted?
  • Competitiveness has been the main angle throughout: about half of the framed sentences in every period and every selection (48–62%).
  • Until early 2022 environmental protection gained ground, peaking in the Green Deal years (2019–22) at 47%, as frequent as competitiveness.
  • Then security came in: after the invasion of Ukraine it at least doubled in every selection, from 4% to 14% including the debates on energy prices and supply, from 4% to 8% in the core climate debates. It began with the energy-price crisis in autumn 2021, peaked in the first half of 2022 (21%) and had faded by September 2023.
  • Security took its room mostly from environmental protection, not from competitiveness. The shift holds within groups and member states, appears in all eight groups, and is strongest in central and eastern Europe.

In one sentence: the Parliament has always argued for the green transition mainly as an economic project; it gave more room to the environmental case under the Green Deal and, from 2022, added a security case, tying energy supply and independence from Russia to the same transition.

What this case teaches

  1. Start from the question, and let it decide what to measure. Each part of the question became a decision: whose discussion, which speeches, which frames, which periods.
  2. Write the definitions down before you measure, including what you leave out and why (nature protection, here).
  3. Read the borderline cases. Every threshold in this study was chosen by reading the texts just above it.
  4. Expect a first attempt to fail. Zero-shot similarity measured the subject, not the frame. Reading its output showed this; a supervised classifier fixed it.
  5. Validate more than the model. The frame classifier was fine; the corpus filter was the weak spot, and only a check on the filter revealed it.
  6. Separate change from composition. A shift in the average can come from a change in who speaks. Fixed effects tell the two apart.
  7. Report ranges when your choices matter. The direction of the shift was robust, its size was not.

Questions for discussion

  1. The Parliament stands in for “the EU”. How might the answer differ for Commission documents, or for the laws themselves? How would you collect those?
  2. Competitiveness includes both an opportunity view (green jobs, innovation) and a burden view (costs, bureaucracy). Split it in two: how would you change the definitions and the labelling? Which of the two do you expect to have grown after 2022?
  3. The labels come from a language model, not from people. Label 100 sentences yourself and compare. Where do you disagree with the model?
  4. Is a debate on energy prices part of “the discussion of the green transition”? Argue both sides. What does your choice do to the answer?
  5. Apply the same design to the Dutch municipal councils: do local politicians frame the transition (wind turbines, heat networks, solar parks) the same way?
