Case study: the changing sustainability orientation of European innovation policy

Topics as a new datapoint, with 7,103 policy initiatives from the EC-OECD STIP survey

Author

Janpieter van der Pol

Most topic analyses stop at a list of topics. This case study treats a topic as a new variable: once every document has one, you can count it, compare it across countries and over time, follow it for the same document, check it against other measures and link it to other data sets. The documents are science, technology and innovation (STI) policy initiatives that EU member states report to the OECD.

NoteThe research question

How do sustainability-related priorities in science, technology and innovation policy differ across EU member states, and how have these priorities evolved across reporting periods?

The question asks for three things: a way to tell whether an initiative is about sustainability, a comparison across member states, and a comparison across reporting periods. Each of these needs a measure that means the same thing in every country and every edition of the survey.

Step 1: the data

The EC-OECD STIP Compass collects national STI policy initiatives through a survey of ministries and agencies. For each initiative it gives a name, a description, objectives and background, the instruments used (grants, strategies, tax relief, …), the target groups, a budget range and theme codes.

Editions 2019 and 2025
Initiatives 7,103 from the 27 member states: 2,807 in 2019, 4,296 in 2025
Followed over time 1,421 initiatives appear in both editions (same identifier)
Text per initiative name + description + objectives + background

Why not use the OECD’s own theme codes? Two reasons. The 2019 edition has no theme code for sustainability, and the 2025 edition added a separate module of questions on net zero, with its own codes (energy, transport, food and agriculture, steel, government capabilities). A count based on codes would therefore compare two different questionnaires. A topic model reads the texts, which are written the same way in both editions.

Choices to notice: only the 2019 and 2025 editions could be downloaded automatically; the 2021 and 2023 editions are on the STIP Data Lab and could be added to the same model. The Belgian regions are merged into Belgium. HTML tags in the texts (<br/> in 28% of them) are removed before anything else: in a first run, br was one of the most frequent topic words.

Step 2: one topic model for both editions

To compare editions, a topic must mean the same thing in 2019 and 2025. We therefore fit one model on all 7,103 initiatives together, not one per edition.

library(Rhobots)
encoder <- load_hf_bert("sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2")
emb <- embed_texts(encoder, corpus$text, max_length = 384)

# try several settings first, and compare the number of topics and their separation
sweep <- sweep_topics(prep_terms(corpus$text), embeddings = emb, n_neighbors = c(10, 15, 30),
                      min_pts = c(10, 15, 25, 40), extra_stopwords = stip_words, seed = 42)
visualize_sweep(sweep)

fit <- fit_bertopic(docs = prep_terms(corpus$text), embeddings = emb, stopwords = sw,
                    umap_n_neighbors = 15, hdbscan_min_pts = 15, seed = 42)
fit <- reduce_outliers(fit)

The setting 15/15 gives 93 topics with good separation (silhouette 0.67). The stopword list sw adds generic policy words (programme, initiative, support, national) and country names to the usual English list; without them, topics group initiatives by country instead of by content.

Labelling the topics, and a lesson on prompting

Ninety-three topics are too many to label by hand, and is this topic about sustainability? is a judgement. A local language model (qwen3 through Ollama) reads each topic’s top terms and its most typical initiatives, and returns a label, a policy domain and a sustainability rating: core (the main purpose), partial (one of several stated aims) or none.

schema <- list(type = "object",
  required = list("purpose", "label", "domain", "sustainability", "green_area"),
  properties = list(
    purpose        = list(type = "string"),                      # first: reason before rating
    label          = list(type = "string"),
    domain         = list(type = "string", enum = names(domains)),
    sustainability = list(type = "string", enum = list("core", "partial", "none")),
    green_area     = list(type = "string", enum = green_areas)))

The first attempt did not work. With the rating first and the explanation after it, the model rated high-performance computing as core sustainability, because computing could help the climate. Putting the purpose field first makes the model describe what the texts say before it judges them, and the instruction judge the stated aims, not what the field could do in principle did the rest. One rating was still corrected by hand (venture capital, rated partial), and the correction is listed in the analysis.

Zooming in

The largest green topic, climate and energy transition strategies, is a mix of everything from hydrogen to adaptation plans. A second topic model, fitted only on the green initiatives, splits them into 19 subtopics, which we group into six areas: energy transition; mobility and cities; industry and circular economy; agri-food, bioeconomy and nature; climate adaptation and environment; oceans and blue economy.

green <- corpus[core == TRUE]
fit_green <- fit_bertopic(docs = prep_terms(green$text), embeddings = emb[green$doc_id, ],
                          stopwords = sw, umap_n_neighbors = 10, hdbscan_min_pts = 8, seed = 42)

This is the topic model used as a microscope: the first model finds the green part of the policy mix, the second shows what it is made of.

Step 3: from topics to a measure

The main measure is simple: the share of initiatives in a core sustainability topic, per edition and per member state. Because one number rests on several choices, we also compute two variants:

  • broad: core plus partial topics;
  • soft: instead of one topic per initiative, a probability for every topic (a softmax over the similarity of the initiative to each topic’s centre); an initiative halfway between a green topic and another counts for half.
# soft topic shares: similarity to each topic centroid, turned into probabilities
S <- emb_unit %*% t(centroids)
P <- exp((S - apply(S, 1, max)) / 0.03); P <- P / rowSums(P)
corpus$soft <- rowSums(P[, topics$sustainability == "core"])

Step 4: checking the measure

Check What it shows
The OECD’s own net-zero tags (2025 only) precision 0.79, recall 0.70, κ = 0.70; per member state the two shares correlate at r = 0.80
A reader: the language model reads 480 random initiatives in full (240 per edition) and judges whether sustainability is the main aim precision 0.85, recall 0.80, κ = 0.80; the reader also finds the rise (4.6% → 18.3%)
Robustness: core, broad and soft variants the same rise and the same ranking of member states

The topic measure and the net-zero tags disagree mostly on agri-food and oceans: these are green topics, but the net-zero module asks about energy, transport and industry. This is a difference in definitions, not an error in either.

Step 5: how the priorities evolved

Measure 2019 2025
Core topics (main measure) 3.5% 13.8%
Core + partial topics 4.3% 15.1%
Soft topic shares 3.3% 12.5%
Reader (480 initiatives) 4.6% 18.3%

Is it a real change, or a different survey?

The 2025 survey asked about net zero; 2019 did not. Two tests:

  • Leave out the 225 initiatives that were reported only under the net-zero module: the share is still 9.7%, nearly three times 2019.
  • If the module only drew out older initiatives that were never reported before, new green initiatives would more often have started before 2019. They did not: 21% of new green initiatives started by 2018, against 24% of other new initiatives.

New policy, or relabelled policy?

Because initiatives keep their identifier, we can split the change into initiatives that ended, continued and started:

Group Initiatives Green share
Reported in 2019 only (ended) 1,386 3.5%
Continued, as described in 2019 1,421 3.6%
Continued, as described in 2025 1,421 4.2%
Reported in 2025 only (new) 2,875 18.5%

Continuing initiatives barely changed (89% kept the same topic). The rise comes from new initiatives, not from old ones described in greener words.

What “sustainability” means shifted

In 2019 the largest green area was agri-food and the bioeconomy (36% of green initiatives); in 2025 it is the energy transition (39%). Hydrogen went from 0 to 51 initiatives, and climate adaptation plans from 11 to 77.

  • The rise is statistically significant in 13 of 27 member states.
  • Central and eastern member states average 7.3% in 2025, against 13.0% elsewhere.
  • The ranking holds under the survey-effect test (rank correlation 0.85 with the lower bound).
  • Priorities differ, not only levels: energy leads in 14 of 26 member states, but Portugal and Malta lead with oceans, Germany and Ireland with agri-food, Sweden with mobility.

Step 7: linking topics to other data

A topic per initiative can be joined to anything else that is known about the initiative or its country.

Within the survey: green initiatives are twice as often strategies or plans (44% against 21% in 2025) and have larger budgets (43% over €20 million a year, against 32%).

Across data sets: we linked each member state’s green share to three outside indicators.

Indicator Source Rank correlation with the green share (2025)
Government R&D budget for environment and energy, 2022–24 Eurostat GBARD 0.16
Climate-tech (Y02) share of patents, 2017–20 PATSTAT 0.07
Environmental policy stringency, 2018–20 OECD EPS −0.12

None is related. A green STI policy mix, as reported to the OECD, does not follow from where a country spends its R&D money, what its firms patent or how strict its environmental rules are. That is a finding, and a question for further research: is the policy mix a statement of intent that budgets and technology have not caught up with, or does it measure something else?

The answer

TipHow do sustainability priorities differ, and how have they evolved?
  • Sustainability has moved from the margin into the core of STI policy: from 3.5% of initiatives in 2019 to 13.8% in 2025, and at least 9.7% when the new net-zero questions are left out.
  • The change comes from new policy: 18.5% of new initiatives are green, while continuing initiatives hardly changed.
  • The meaning of sustainability shifted from agri-food and the bioeconomy to the energy transition, with hydrogen and climate adaptation as new priorities.
  • Member states differ widely, from none (Slovakia) to almost a quarter (Spain, Portugal), with central and eastern member states lagging, and with different priorities: oceans in Portugal and Malta, agri-food in Germany and Ireland, mobility in Sweden.
  • The policy mix is not explained by green R&D budgets, climate-tech patents or environmental policy stringency.

What topics as a datapoint make possible

  1. A consistent measure where the official codes change. One model on both editions gives a variable that means the same in 2019 and 2025, which the survey’s own codes do not.
  2. Any level of detail. A second model on a subset (here the green initiatives) works as a microscope.
  3. Change for the same unit. With a stable identifier, topics show whether a change is new policy or old policy described differently.
  4. Validation. A topic variable can be checked against other codes and against a reader, like any measure.
  5. Linking. A topic per document joins to instruments, budgets, and to other data sets (R&D budgets, patents, regulation).

What this case teaches

  1. Prefer the texts over the codes when the codes change. The questionnaire changed between editions; the way initiatives are described did not.
  2. Fit one model when you want to compare. Separate models per edition give topics that cannot be matched.
  3. Let a language model reason before it rates. The order of the fields in the schema changed the ratings.
  4. Ask how the data were collected. A new survey module can create a trend by itself; the lower bound and the start-year test address that.
  5. Report variants, not one number. Core, broad, soft and reader give different levels but the same story.

Questions for discussion

  1. The measure counts initiatives, not money. A small grant and a national strategy weigh the same. How would you weight them, and what might change?
  2. Spain went from 0.7% to 23.6%. Is that a change in policy, or in who filled in the survey? How could you find out?
  3. Agri-food and oceans count as sustainability here, but not in the OECD’s net-zero module. Which definition fits the research question better?
  4. The green share is unrelated to green R&D budgets. Think of three explanations, and of a data set that would tell them apart.
  5. Download the 2021 and 2023 editions from the STIP Data Lab and add them to the same model. Is the rise gradual, or does it start with the Green Deal (2019) or the energy crisis (2022)?
