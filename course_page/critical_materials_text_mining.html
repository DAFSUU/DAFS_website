Critical raw materials in patent texts

A text-mining case study in R: from 60 GB of patent text to materials, uses and themes

Author

Janpieter van der Pol

1 Why look for materials in patents?

A smartphone contains around 30 different elements. An electric car battery needs lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese and graphite. A wind turbine generator needs neodymium and dysprosium for its magnets. Most of these materials are mined and refined in only a few countries: most cobalt comes from the Democratic Republic of Congo, and most rare-earth refining happens in China. Europe imports almost all of them.

For that reason the European Union keeps a list of critical raw materials: materials that matter for the economy and whose supply is at risk. The 2024 Critical Raw Materials Act lists 34 of them, and marks 17 as strategic because they are needed for the green and digital transitions. Governments now want to know:

  • Who has the knowledge to use these materials? Which regions and firms invent batteries, magnets or catalysts?
  • Where is that knowledge compared with the supply? Does Europe invent where it also has deposits, or does it depend on others for both?
  • How are the materials used, and can they be replaced? Is cobalt used in batteries, alloys or catalysts? Which patents try to use less cobalt, and what takes its place?

Patents are a good source for these questions. Every patent describes an invention in detail, and the inventors’ addresses tell us where it was made. But patent classification codes say what an invention does (“electrode for a lithium battery”), not which materials it uses. That information sits in the text: the description and the claims. To get it out, we need text mining.

NoteWhat you will learn

This page walks through a real research pipeline, applied to the full text of all European patent publications from mid-2013 to mid-2022: about 60 GB of text. You will see how to:

  1. build a dictionary of materials: names in three languages, element symbols and chemical formulas;
  2. clean XML text and split it into sentences;
  3. find materials with regular expressions, and keep the sentence before and after each mention as context;
  4. measure and reduce false positives, and score how relevant a mention is;
  5. classify how a material is used, with cue words and with a local large language model (LLM);
  6. find themes with a topic model (BERTopic).

Steps 1–4 come with small R examples that you can run yourself. Steps 5–6 show the code that was used, without running it here, because it needs the full data and a language model.

1.1 The data

The analysis combines the patent texts with ten other sources: patent records, inventor and applicant addresses, mineral deposits, production, trade and regional data. This page focuses on the patent texts. The other sources come in when we link the results to places.

The text comes from the EPO’s full-text data for text analytics: one large tab-separated file per block of 100,000 EP publication numbers. Each line holds one text part of one publication in one language:

country  pub_number  kind  pub_date    lang  text_type  version  text
EP       3653582     A1    2020-05-20  en    CLAIM      1        <claim id="c-en-0001" num="0001"><claim-text>A positive electrode ...

The text_type is the title (TITLE), abstract (ABSTR), description (DESCR) or claims (CLAIM). The text itself contains XML markup: paragraphs, claims, sub- and superscripts (LiCoO<sub>2</sub>), tables and formulas.

2 Results

Before we look at how it works, let’s look at what comes out of it.

2.1 How often are critical materials mentioned?

Of the 1,035,784 EP applications with full text, 442,470 (43%) mention at least one critical raw material. That sounds like a lot, and it is: many of those mentions are incidental. An aluminium housing, copper wiring or a list of “suitable metals” is not what the patent is about.

So every mention is scored for relevance:

  • strong: the material is named in the title, abstract or claims, outside a list of elements;
  • weak: it is only named in the description;
  • list only: it is only named in long lists such as “selected from Co, Rh, Ir, Fe, Ni, Pt, Pd…”.

Only 137,885 applications (13%) mention a critical material strongly. Figure 1 shows how this differs per material.

  • Common metals are mostly background. Aluminium, silicon, copper and titanium appear in hundreds of thousands of patents, but in only 15–27% of them in the title, abstract or claims.

  • The way you count decides the result. Before you compare materials, decide which relevance level answers your question.

3 Finding materials in text

The rest of this page explains how the results above were made. The code examples use two packages: data.table for tables and stringi for text and regular expressions.

3.1 Step 1: a dictionary of materials

Text mining starts with knowing what you are looking for. The dictionary has one row per material: the 34 materials on the EU list, with the rare earths and platinum-group metals split into single elements (54 rows in total). The names are regular expressions, not plain words. lithi(?:um|at) matches lithium and lithiated; platin(?:um|ic|ous|i[sz]ed|ate) matches platinum and platinized but not platform.

Some choices need domain knowledge:

  • In German, words are glued together (Lithiumeisenphosphat, Polysilizium), so German names are matched anywhere inside a word. In English and French a name must start a word.
  • German Platine means a circuit board, and French platine can also mean a mounting plate. Bore is French for boron but English for a hole.
  • Bare symbols are switched off (bare_symbol = FALSE) when they are also common words or abbreviations: Co (company), W (watt), V (volt), P (pressure), Y (a variable), B, As, Be…

3.2 Step 2: cleaning the text

The raw text contains XML markup. We remove it, but carefully: LiCoO<sub>2</sub> must become LiCoO2, not LiCoO 2. So inline tags (sub, sup, italics, bold) are removed without leaving a space, and block tags (paragraphs, headings, claims) are replaced by a space or a break.

raw <- paste0('<p id="p0012" num="0012">The cathode comprises LiNi<sub>0.8</sub>Co<sub>0.1</sub>',
              'Mn<sub>0.1</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, as shown in Fig. 2. Cobalt is expensive and its supply is ',
              'concentrated. The applicant, ACME Co., Ltd., tested it with switch SW1 on the PCB. ',
              'Lu et al. measured a capacity of 200 mAh/g with Li metal as counter electrode.</p>')

txt <- stri_replace_all_regex(raw, "</?(?:sub|sup|i|b)>", "")          # inline tags: no space
txt <- stri_trim_both(stri_replace_all_regex(txt, "<[^>]+>", " "))       # other tags: a space
txt
[1] "The cathode comprises LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2, as shown in Fig. 2. Cobalt is expensive and its supply is concentrated. The applicant, ACME Co., Ltd., tested it with switch SW1 on the PCB. Lu et al. measured a capacity of 200 mAh/g with Li metal as counter electrode."

The full pipeline also keeps the paragraph number (num="0012"), decodes HTML entities (&#945; → α) and drops mathematical formulas and images.

3.3 Step 3: splitting into sentences

We want to keep each mention with its sentence and the sentences around it, so we need sentences. The ICU library (used by stringi) knows the sentence rules of many languages:

s <- stri_split_boundaries(txt, type = "sentence")[[1]]
s
[1] "The cathode comprises LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2, as shown in Fig. "                  
[2] "2. "                                                                           
[3] "Cobalt is expensive and its supply is concentrated. "                          
[4] "The applicant, ACME Co., Ltd., tested it with switch SW1 on the PCB. "         
[5] "Lu et al. measured a capacity of 200 mAh/g with Li metal as counter electrode."

ICU splits after “Fig.” because it looks like the end of a sentence. Scientific and legal text is full of such abbreviations: Fig., e.g., Co., Ltd., et al., wt.%. A simple fix is to glue a piece to the next one when it ends in a known abbreviation:

abbr_end <- "(?i)(?:^|\\s)(?:figs?|e\\.g|i\\.e|no|co|ltd|al)\\.\\s*$"

merge_abbrev <- function(s) {
  out <- character()
  for (x in s) {
    if (length(out) && stri_detect_regex(out[length(out)], abbr_end)) {
      out[length(out)] <- paste0(out[length(out)], x)   # glue to the previous piece
    } else {
      out <- c(out, x)
    }
  }
  stri_trim_both(out)
}

sent <- merge_abbrev(s)
sent
[1] "The cathode comprises LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2, as shown in Fig. 2."

3.4 Step 4: matching names, symbols and formulas

3.4.1 Names

Names are the easy part: a case-insensitive regular expression per material and language. (?<!\p{L}) means “not preceded by a letter”, so the name must start a word.

m[, .(sentence = which(stri_detect_regex(sent, sprintf("(?i)(?<!\\p{L})(?:%s)", regex_en)))),
  by = material_id]

Symbols are case-sensitive (Li, not LI or li) and must stand alone. A naive pattern finds too much:

stri_extract_all_regex(sent, "\\b(?:Li|Co|Ni|Mn|Lu)\\b")

“Co” in ACME Co., Ltd. is a company and “Lu” in Lu et al. is an author. In the full data, 10–20% of the standalone Li, Lu and Ho matches were author names. Lookarounds let the pattern check what comes before and after the symbol without including it in the match:

better <- "(?<!\\p{L})(?:Li|Lu)(?!\\p{L})(?!\\s*,?\\s*et\\s+al)"   # not followed by "et al"
stri_extract_all_regex(sent, better)

[[2]]
[1] NA

[[3]]
[1] NA

The full pipeline adds more lookarounds: no “J. Li”, no “Li, K.”, no “Fung M, Lu M”. Co is not searched as a bare symbol at all.

3.4.3 Chemical formulas

Formulas carry much of the information in chemistry and materials patents. They are found in two steps: first collect candidates (two or more capitalised groups, with optional numbers), then check them.

elem_re <- paste(elements[order(-nchar(elements))], collapse = "|")   # longest first: "Co" before "C"

cand <- unlist(stri_extract_all_regex(
  sent, "(?<![\\p{L}_])(?:[A-Z][a-z]?[0-9.]*){2,}(?![\\p{L}\\p{N}_])", omit_no_match = TRUE))

is_formula <- function(tok) {
  only_elements <- stri_detect_regex(tok, sprintf("^(?:%s|[0-9.])+$", elem_re))
  syms          <- stri_extract_all_regex(tok, elem_re)
  two_letter    <- vapply(syms, function(x) any(nchar(x) == 2), logical(1))
  chem_digit    <- stri_detect_regex(tok, "[A-Z][0-9]+[A-Z]|[OFH][2-9]$")   # V2O5, WO3
  only_elements & (two_letter | chem_digit)
}

data.table(token        = cand,
           all_elements = stri_detect_regex(cand, sprintf("^(?:%s|[0-9.])+$", elem_re)),

A dictionary search always finds things that are not there. The only way to find out is to read samples of the matches and count the errors per material and per kind of match. In this project, that revealed:

Each problem gets a rule: a stop list, a pattern (a symbol followed directly by an index, as in Tb7), or a language-specific check. Because every match was saved with its sentence, the rules can be applied after the search, without reading the 60 GB again. The effect was large for some materials: −22% for yttrium, −17% for vanadium, −13% for praseodymium and −9% for platinum, but close to zero for aluminium, copper or lithium.

Then each publication × material pair is scored for relevance (Figure 1). An element list is a sentence that matches five or more materials and has four or more separators (commas, “or”, “and”), without quantities: “0.5 wt% Nb, 0.1% V…” is an alloy composition, not a list.

tags[, relevance := fifelse(n_main > 0, "strong", fifelse(n_other > 0, "weak", "list_only"))]
ImportantPrecision and recall

Every rule that removes false positives can also remove true ones: switching off the symbol Co means we miss “a Co layer”. A dictionary approach trades recall (finding everything) against precision (finding only real mentions). Measure both on a hand-coded sample, per material, before you trust a count.

3.7 Scaling up

The examples above run on one paragraph. The real data has 15 files of up to 9.5 GB. Three techniques make that manageable:

  • Read in chunks. readLines(con, n = 1000) reads 1,000 lines at a time, so memory use stays low no matter how big the file is.
  • Work in parallel. Each file, and each chunk within a file, can be processed on its own core with parallel::mclapply().
  • Make it resumable. After a file is done, a small marker file stores its name, size and date. A new run skips finished files, so you can add files later or restart after a crash.
con <- file("2022week30_EP3600000.txt", open = "r", encoding = "UTF-8")
repeat {
  lines <- readLines(con, n = 1000)
  if (!length(lines)) break
  res <- process_chunk(lines)          # parse, clean, split, match, keep context
  fwrite(res$contexts, "contexts.csv", append = file.exists("contexts.csv"))

4 How is a material used?

Knowing that a patent mentions cobalt is a start. For policy, the use matters: cobalt in a battery cathode faces different risks and alternatives than cobalt in a superalloy or a catalyst. The context sentences make it possible to classify the use.

4.1 Cue words

The first approach is a second dictionary: cue words per use. Each context sentence is scored for eleven uses: catalysts, magnets, batteries, alloys, semiconductors, coatings, conductors, glass and ceramics, pharma, chemicals and structural parts. A simplified version:

cues <- list(
  battery   = "batter(?:y|ies)|cathode|anode|electrolyte|secondary cell|lithium[- ]ion",
  magnet    = "magnet|coercivit|remanence|rotor|ndfeb",
  catalyst  = "cataly[sz]",
  alloy     = "alloy|steel|superalloy|weld|solder",
  coating   = "coating|plating|deposited layer|film"
)

scores <- contexts[, lapply(cues, function(p) {
  w <- fifelse(section %in% c("title", "abstract", "claims"), 2, 1) *   # main sections count double
       fifelse(in_list, 0.25, 1)                                        # lists count a quarter
  sum(w * stri_detect_regex(paste(prev_sentence, sentence, next_sentence), paste0("(?i)", p)))
}), by = .(pub_key, material_id)]

uses <- melt(scores, id.vars = c("pub_key", "material_id"), variable.name = "use")
uses <- uses[value > 0][order(-value), .SD[1], by = .(pub_key, material_id)]   # main use

When no cue is found, the patent’s IPC classification decides (for example class H01M is batteries). This way 81% of the 1.9 million patent–material pairs get a use. The cue words are English only, so German and French patents depend more on the IPC codes.

Cue words are transparent and fast, but they cannot answer open questions such as which problem does this patent solve? or what replaces cobalt here? For that we used a language model.

5 Using a local language model

A large language model (LLM) can read a context and answer a question about it in a fixed format. We used local models that run on a laptop with Ollama, called from R with the rollama package. A local model costs nothing per query, and the texts never leave your computer.

Three tasks used an LLM:

  1. Does the patent aim to use less cobalt? The LLM reads the cobalt contexts and decides; rules then clean up the answers.
  2. Which problem does the patent solve? For cobalt patents, the LLM names the problem in a few words (cost, thermal stability, supply…).
  3. What replaces cobalt? For the 132 patents that aim to use less cobalt, Mistral Small 3.2 names the elements that take cobalt’s place, with a verbatim quote from the text.
# once, in a terminal:  ollama pull mistral-small3.2
library(rollama)

system_prompt <- "
You read sentences from a patent that mention cobalt.
Answer in JSON with these fields:
  aims_less_cobalt: true if the invention aims to use less cobalt or none, else false
  replacement:      list of chemical elements that take the place of cobalt, [] if none
  quote:            the exact words from the text that support your answer
Only use information that is in the text."

ask_llm <- function(text, model = "mistral-small3.2") {
  msgs <- data.frame(role = c("system", "user"), content = c(system_prompt, text))
  out  <- query(msgs, model = model, screen = FALSE, output = "text", format = "json",
                model_params = list(temperature = 0, seed = 42))
  jsonlite::fromJSON(out)
}

# one call per patent: its cobalt context sentences joined together
ask_llm(paste(ctx$prev_sentence, ctx$sentence, ctx$next_sentence, collapse = " "))

Some practical lessons:

  • Ask for JSON (format = "json") and fixed answer categories, so the output can be parsed into a table.
  • Set temperature = 0 so the same input gives the same answer.
  • Ask for a quote, and check it. A model can make things up. If the quote is not in the text, the answer is thrown away. An element named as a replacement counts only if it is also named in the patent:
ans$quote_found <- stri_detect_fixed(patent_text, ans$quote)
ans$in_text     <- vapply(ans$replacement, function(el) stri_detect_regex(patent_text, el), logical(1))
  • Validate by hand. Read a random sample of answers before using them. In this project, a first check of 14 replacement answers found about a quarter doubtful (for example nitrogen named as a replacement for cobalt).

6 Finding themes with a topic model

Cue words and LLM questions only find what we look for. A topic model works the other way round: it groups patents that are about similar things and tells us what each group is about. For cobalt, the largest themes include “cobalt-based turbine component coatings”, “lithium cobalt oxide battery materials”, “cemented carbide cutting tools”, “Fischer-Tropsch hydrocarbon synthesis” and even “endovascular valve prostheses”: finer than any category we would set in advance.

We used BERTopic, which works in four steps:

  1. Embed: each title and abstract becomes a vector of numbers, using a language model trained on patents (BERT for Patents). Texts about similar things get similar vectors.
  2. Reduce: UMAP squeezes the 1,024 dimensions to a handful, keeping close texts close.
  3. Cluster: HDBSCAN finds dense groups of texts. Each group is a topic. Texts that fit no group are assigned to the nearest one.
  4. Describe: class-based TF-IDF finds the words that are typical for each topic, and a local LLM writes a short label from those words and three typical abstracts.

The model was run separately for 47 materials (about 131,000 patents with a strong mention and an English abstract), with Rhobots, an R package that runs the whole BERTopic pipeline in R:

library(Rhobots)

enc  <- load_hf_bert("anferico/bert-for-patents")
docs <- paste(pat$title, pat$abstract, sep = ". ")        # patents strongly naming one material
emb  <- embed_texts_cached(enc, docs, cache_file = "emb_cobalt.rds", normalize = TRUE)

fit <- fit_bertopic(docs, embeddings = emb,
                    umap_n_neighbors  = 15L,
                    umap_n_components = 5L,
                    hdbscan_min_pts   = max(10L, length(docs) %/% 300L),   # grows with the corpus
                    ngram_range       = c(1L, 2L),
                    reduce_frequent_words = TRUE,
                    seed = 42L)
fit <- reduce_outliers(fit, strategy = "embeddings", threshold = 0)       # no "noise" topic
fit <- label_topics_llm(fit, provider = "ollama", model = "mistral-small3.2",
                        top_n_terms = 10L, n_representative_docs = 3L)

Once each patent has a topic, the topic can be joined to the inventors’ regions and the applicants. That is what the topic map in Section 2 shows: per theme, where it is invented and by whom, and whether it grew between 2010–15 and 2016–21.

NoteDictionary or topic model?

The two approaches answer different questions. A dictionary answers “where is X mentioned?” with high precision, if you know what to look for. A topic model answers “what is in here?” without knowing in advance. The topics depend on choices (the embedding model, the minimum topic size), and their labels are written by a model, so read the typical patents of each topic before you trust its label.

7 Wrapping up

From 60 GB of patent text to maps of knowledge and supply, the pipeline went through these steps:

  1. Dictionary: names in three languages, symbols and formulas, with domain knowledge about false friends.
  2. Clean and split: remove markup without breaking formulas, and split sentences around abbreviations.
  3. Match and keep context: regular expressions with lookarounds; the sentence before and after each mention.
  4. Check precision: read samples, write rules, measure again; score relevance.
  5. Classify uses: cue words for broad uses, an LLM for open questions, always with a check.
  6. Find themes: a topic model on the patents that are really about a material.
  7. Link to places: inventor regions, applicants and deposits.

The most important lesson is not technical: look at your matches. Every count on this page changed after we read samples of what was found.

# Why look for materials in patents? {#sec-why}

A smartphone contains around 30 different elements. An electric car battery needs lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese and graphite. A wind turbine generator needs neodymium and dysprosium for its magnets. Most of these materials are mined and refined in only a few countries: most cobalt comes from the Democratic Republic of Congo, and most rare-earth refining happens in China. Europe imports almost all of them.

For that reason the European Union keeps a list of **critical raw materials**: materials that matter for the economy and whose supply is at risk. The 2024 *Critical Raw Materials Act* lists 34 of them, and marks 17 as **strategic** because they are needed for the green and digital transitions. Governments now want to know:

- **Who has the knowledge to use these materials?** Which regions and firms invent batteries, magnets or catalysts?
- **Where is that knowledge compared with the supply?** Does Europe invent where it also has deposits, or does it depend on others for both?
- **How are the materials used, and can they be replaced?** Is cobalt used in batteries, alloys or catalysts? Which patents try to use *less* cobalt, and what takes its place?

Patents are a good source for these questions. Every patent describes an invention in detail, and the inventors' addresses tell us where it was made. But patent **classification codes** say what an invention does ("electrode for a lithium battery"), not which materials it uses. That information sits in the **text**: the description and the claims. To get it out, we need text mining.

## Step 1: a dictionary of materials {#sec-dictionary}

Text mining starts with knowing what you are looking for. The dictionary has one row per material: the 34 materials on the EU list, with the rare earths and platinum-group metals split into single elements (54 rows in total). A material can appear in a text in three ways, so the dictionary has a column for each:

| Form | Example | Column |
|---|---|---|
| Name, per language | *cobalt*, *Kobalt*, *cobalt* (fr); *lithium iron phosphate*, *Lithiumeisenphosphat* | `regex_en`, `regex_de`, `regex_fr` |
| Element symbol | Co, Li, Nd | `symbol`, `bare_symbol` |
| Chemical formula | LiCoO<sub>2</sub>, Nd<sub>2</sub>Fe<sub>14</sub>B, BaSO<sub>4</sub> | found from the symbols, plus `formula_literals` for minerals |

```{r}
#| label: dictionary
materials <- fread("data/crm_materials.csv", encoding = "UTF-8")
materials[material_id %in% c("cobalt", "lithium", "neodymium", "platinum", "baryte"),
          .(material_id, symbol, bare_symbol, regex_en, regex_de, formula_literals)]
```

The names are **regular expressions**, not plain words. `lithi(?:um|at)` matches *lithium* and *lithiated*; `platin(?:um|ic|ous|i[sz]ed|ate)` matches *platinum* and *platinized* but not *platform*.

Some choices need domain knowledge:

- In **German**, words are glued together (*Lithiumeisenphosphat*, *Polysilizium*), so German names are matched anywhere inside a word. In English and French a name must start a word.
- German **Platine** means a circuit board, and French **platine** can also mean a mounting plate. *Bore* is French for boron but English for a hole.
- **Bare symbols** are switched off (`bare_symbol = FALSE`) when they are also common words or abbreviations: *Co* (company), *W* (watt), *V* (volt), *P* (pressure), *Y* (a variable), *B*, *As*, *Be*…

## Step 2: cleaning the text {#sec-cleaning}

The raw text contains XML markup. We remove it, but carefully: `LiCoO<sub>2</sub>` must become `LiCoO2`, not `LiCoO 2`. So **inline tags** (sub, sup, italics, bold) are removed without leaving a space, and **block tags** (paragraphs, headings, claims) are replaced by a space or a break.

```{r}
#| label: clean
raw <- paste0('<p id="p0012" num="0012">The cathode comprises LiNi<sub>0.8</sub>Co<sub>0.1</sub>',
              'Mn<sub>0.1</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, as shown in Fig. 2. Cobalt is expensive and its supply is ',
              'concentrated. The applicant, ACME Co., Ltd., tested it with switch SW1 on the PCB. ',
              'Lu et al. measured a capacity of 200 mAh/g with Li metal as counter electrode.</p>')

txt <- stri_replace_all_regex(raw, "</?(?:sub|sup|i|b)>", "")          # inline tags: no space
txt <- stri_trim_both(stri_replace_all_regex(txt, "<[^>]+>", " "))       # other tags: a space
txt
```

The full pipeline also keeps the paragraph number (`num="0012"`), decodes HTML entities (`&#945;` → α) and drops mathematical formulas and images.

## Step 3: splitting into sentences {#sec-sentences}

We want to keep each mention with its sentence and the sentences around it, so we need sentences. The ICU library (used by stringi) knows the sentence rules of many languages:

```{r}
#| label: split
s <- stri_split_boundaries(txt, type = "sentence")[[1]]
s
```

ICU splits after "Fig." because it looks like the end of a sentence. Scientific and legal text is full of such abbreviations: *Fig.*, *e.g.*, *Co., Ltd.*, *et al.*, *wt.%*. A simple fix is to glue a piece to the next one when it ends in a known abbreviation:

```{r}
#| label: merge
abbr_end <- "(?i)(?:^|\\s)(?:figs?|e\\.g|i\\.e|no|co|ltd|al)\\.\\s*$"

merge_abbrev <- function(s) {
  out <- character()
  for (x in s) {
    if (length(out) && stri_detect_regex(out[length(out)], abbr_end)) {
      out[length(out)] <- paste0(out[length(out)], x)   # glue to the previous piece
    } else {
      out <- c(out, x)
    }
  }
  stri_trim_both(out)
}

sent <- merge_abbrev(s)
sent
```

The full pipeline also glues a piece when the next one starts with a lower-case letter, and uses a longer list of abbreviations in English, German and French.

## Step 4: matching names, symbols and formulas {#sec-matching}

### Names

Names are the easy part: a case-insensitive regular expression per material and language. `(?<!\p{L})` means "not preceded by a letter", so the name must start a word.

```{r}
#| label: names
m <- materials[material_id %in% c("lithium", "cobalt", "nickel", "manganese", "lutetium")]
m[, .(sentence = which(stri_detect_regex(sent, sprintf("(?i)(?<!\\p{L})(?:%s)", regex_en)))),
  by = material_id]
```

Only cobalt is found by name: the first sentence names four materials, but only as a formula.

### Element symbols

Symbols are case-sensitive (*Li*, not *LI* or *li*) and must stand alone. A naive pattern finds too much:

```{r}
#| label: symbols-naive
stri_extract_all_regex(sent, "\\b(?:Li|Co|Ni|Mn|Lu)\\b")
```

"Co" in *ACME Co., Ltd.* is a company and "Lu" in *Lu et al.* is an author. In the full data, 10–20% of the standalone *Li*, *Lu* and *Ho* matches were author names. **Lookarounds** let the pattern check what comes before and after the symbol without including it in the match:

```{r}
#| label: symbols-better
better <- "(?<!\\p{L})(?:Li|Lu)(?!\\p{L})(?!\\s*,?\\s*et\\s+al)"   # not followed by "et al"
stri_extract_all_regex(sent, better)
```

The full pipeline adds more lookarounds: no "J. Li", no "Li, K.", no "Fung M, Lu M". *Co* is not searched as a bare symbol at all.

### Chemical formulas

Formulas carry much of the information in chemistry and materials patents. They are found in two steps: first collect **candidates** (two or more capitalised groups, with optional numbers), then **check** them.

```{r}
#| label: formulas
elements <- c("H","He","Li","Be","B","C","N","O","F","Ne","Na","Mg","Al","Si","P","S","Cl","Ar",
  "K","Ca","Sc","Ti","V","Cr","Mn","Fe","Co","Ni","Cu","Zn","Ga","Ge","As","Se","Br","Kr","Rb",
  "Sr","Y","Zr","Nb","Mo","Tc","Ru","Rh","Pd","Ag","Cd","In","Sn","Sb","Te","I","Xe","Cs","Ba",
  "La","Ce","Pr","Nd","Pm","Sm","Eu","Gd","Tb","Dy","Ho","Er","Tm","Yb","Lu","Hf","Ta","W","Re",
  "Os","Ir","Pt","Au","Hg","Tl","Pb","Bi","Th","U")
elem_re <- paste(elements[order(-nchar(elements))], collapse = "|")   # longest first: "Co" before "C"

cand <- unlist(stri_extract_all_regex(
  sent, "(?<![\\p{L}_])(?:[A-Z][a-z]?[0-9.]*){2,}(?![\\p{L}\\p{N}_])", omit_no_match = TRUE))

is_formula <- function(tok) {
  only_elements <- stri_detect_regex(tok, sprintf("^(?:%s|[0-9.])+$", elem_re))
  syms          <- stri_extract_all_regex(tok, elem_re)
  two_letter    <- vapply(syms, function(x) any(nchar(x) == 2), logical(1))
  chem_digit    <- stri_detect_regex(tok, "[A-Z][0-9]+[A-Z]|[OFH][2-9]$")   # V2O5, WO3
  only_elements & (two_letter | chem_digit)
}

data.table(token        = cand,
           all_elements = stri_detect_regex(cand, sprintf("^(?:%s|[0-9.])+$", elem_re)),
           is_formula   = is_formula(cand))
```

*SW1* and *PCB* consist only of element symbols (S, W; P, C, B), yet they are not formulas. The rule "at least one two-letter symbol, or a digit between capitals" removes them, while keeping V<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub> and WO<sub>3</sub>. Once a token is accepted, the symbols inside it tell us which materials it contains:

```{r}
#| label: formula-symbols
stri_extract_all_regex("LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2", elem_re)[[1]]
```

So one formula tags a patent with lithium, nickel, cobalt and manganese.

## Step 5: keeping the context {#sec-context}

A mention alone tells us little. The sentence before and after usually say what the material is for. With the sentences in a table, `data.table::shift()` gives the neighbours:

```{r}
#| label: context
S <- data.table(para = "0012", sent_id = seq_along(sent), sentence = sent)
S[, `:=`(prev_sentence = shift(sentence, 1), next_sentence = shift(sentence, -1))]
S[stri_detect_regex(sentence, "(?i)cobalt|Co[0-9]"), .(sent_id, prev_sentence, sentence, next_sentence)]
```

In the full pipeline, the neighbours are taken within the same text part (description, claims…). Every match becomes one row: publication, section, language, paragraph, material, how it was found (name, symbol or formula), the matched terms, and the three sentences. These are real examples from the output:

```{r}
ex <- fread("data/example_contexts.csv", encoding = "UTF-8")
ex[, .(pub_key, material_id, matched_terms, in_list, prev_sentence, sentence, next_sentence)]
```

## Step 6: false positives and relevance {#sec-precision}

A dictionary search always finds things that are not there. The only way to find out is to **read samples** of the matches and count the errors per material and per kind of match. In this project, that revealed:

| Problem | Examples | Counted as |
|---|---|---|
| Plural acronyms | NOs, I/Os, FIFOs | osmium (Os) |
| Viruses, genes, enzymes | SARS-CoV-2, PI3K, CYP2C9, HBeAg | cobalt + vanadium, phosphorus, yttrium, beryllium |
| Protein mutations, step labels | V205I, S228P, S1B, P2Y | vanadium, phosphorus, boron, yttrium |
| Telecom and IT terms | V2I (vehicle-to-infrastructure), SeNB, ReLU | vanadium, boron, lutetium |
| Oxidation states | Cr(VI), Ti(IV) | vanadium (the Roman numeral V) |
| Variables | "time Tb elapses", signal Sc2, Ir1 | terbium, scandium, iridium |
| Words in other languages | French *Si* ("if"), *platine* ("plate") | silicon, platinum |
| Author names | Lu et al., Li K. | lutetium, lithium |

Each problem gets a rule: a stop list, a pattern (a symbol followed directly by an index, as in *Tb7*), or a language-specific check. Because every match was saved with its sentence, the rules can be applied **after** the search, without reading the 60 GB again. The effect was large for some materials: −22% for yttrium, −17% for vanadium, −13% for praseodymium and −9% for platinum, but close to zero for aluminium, copper or lithium.

Then each publication × material pair is scored for relevance (@fig-materials). An **element list** is a sentence that matches five or more materials and has four or more separators (commas, "or", "and"), without quantities: "0.5 wt% Nb, 0.1% V…" is an alloy composition, not a list.

```{r}
#| label: relevance
#| eval: false
# per publication x material, from the context rows
tags <- contexts[, .(n_main  = sum(!in_list & section %in% c("title", "abstract", "claims")),
                     n_other = sum(!in_list)),
                 by = .(pub_key, material_id)]
tags[, relevance := fifelse(n_main > 0, "strong", fifelse(n_other > 0, "weak", "list_only"))]
```

::: {.callout-important}
## Precision and recall

Every rule that removes false positives can also remove true ones: switching off the symbol *Co* means we miss "a Co layer". A dictionary approach trades **recall** (finding everything) against **precision** (finding only real mentions). Measure both on a hand-coded sample, per material, before you trust a count.
:::

## Scaling up {#sec-scaling}

The examples above run on one paragraph. The real data has 15 files of up to 9.5 GB. Three techniques make that manageable:

- **Read in chunks.** `readLines(con, n = 1000)` reads 1,000 lines at a time, so memory use stays low no matter how big the file is.
- **Work in parallel.** Each file, and each chunk within a file, can be processed on its own core with `parallel::mclapply()`.
- **Make it resumable.** After a file is done, a small marker file stores its name, size and date. A new run skips finished files, so you can add files later or restart after a crash.

```{r}
#| label: chunks
#| eval: false
con <- file("2022week30_EP3600000.txt", open = "r", encoding = "UTF-8")
repeat {
  lines <- readLines(con, n = 1000)
  if (!length(lines)) break
  res <- process_chunk(lines)          # parse, clean, split, match, keep context
  fwrite(res$contexts, "contexts.csv", append = file.exists("contexts.csv"))
}
close(con)
```

On 8 cores the full run took about two hours per batch of files. One full-text file of 9 GB became about 1 GB of context sentences: `r fmt(k[["context_rows"]])` rows in total.

# How is a material used? {#sec-uses}

Knowing that a patent mentions cobalt is a start. For policy, the **use** matters: cobalt in a battery cathode faces different risks and alternatives than cobalt in a superalloy or a catalyst. The context sentences make it possible to classify the use.

## Cue words

The first approach is a second dictionary: **cue words per use**. Each context sentence is scored for eleven uses: catalysts, magnets, batteries, alloys, semiconductors, coatings, conductors, glass and ceramics, pharma, chemicals and structural parts. A simplified version:

```{r}
#| label: cues
#| eval: false
cues <- list(
  battery   = "batter(?:y|ies)|cathode|anode|electrolyte|secondary cell|lithium[- ]ion",
  magnet    = "magnet|coercivit|remanence|rotor|ndfeb",
  catalyst  = "cataly[sz]",
  alloy     = "alloy|steel|superalloy|weld|solder",
  coating   = "coating|plating|deposited layer|film"
)

scores <- contexts[, lapply(cues, function(p) {
  w <- fifelse(section %in% c("title", "abstract", "claims"), 2, 1) *   # main sections count double
       fifelse(in_list, 0.25, 1)                                        # lists count a quarter
  sum(w * stri_detect_regex(paste(prev_sentence, sentence, next_sentence), paste0("(?i)", p)))
}), by = .(pub_key, material_id)]

uses <- melt(scores, id.vars = c("pub_key", "material_id"), variable.name = "use")
uses <- uses[value > 0][order(-value), .SD[1], by = .(pub_key, material_id)]   # main use
```

When no cue is found, the patent's IPC classification decides (for example class H01M is batteries). This way 81% of the 1.9 million patent–material pairs get a use. The cue words are English only, so German and French patents depend more on the IPC codes.

Cue words are transparent and fast, but they cannot answer open questions such as *which problem does this patent solve?* or *what replaces cobalt here?* For that we used a language model.

# Using a local language model {#sec-llm}

A **large language model (LLM)** can read a context and answer a question about it in a fixed format. We used **local** models that run on a laptop with [Ollama](https://ollama.com), called from R with the [rollama](https://jbgruber.github.io/rollama/) package. A local model costs nothing per query, and the texts never leave your computer.

Three tasks used an LLM:

1. **Does the patent aim to use less cobalt?** The LLM reads the cobalt contexts and decides; rules then clean up the answers.
2. **Which problem does the patent solve?** For cobalt patents, the LLM names the problem in a few words (cost, thermal stability, supply…).
3. **What replaces cobalt?** For the 132 patents that aim to use less cobalt, *Mistral Small 3.2* names the elements that take cobalt's place, **with a verbatim quote** from the text.

```{r}
#| label: llm-setup
#| eval: false
# once, in a terminal:  ollama pull mistral-small3.2
library(rollama)

system_prompt <- "
You read sentences from a patent that mention cobalt.
Answer in JSON with these fields:
  aims_less_cobalt: true if the invention aims to use less cobalt or none, else false
  replacement:      list of chemical elements that take the place of cobalt, [] if none
  quote:            the exact words from the text that support your answer
Only use information that is in the text."

ask_llm <- function(text, model = "mistral-small3.2") {
  msgs <- data.frame(role = c("system", "user"), content = c(system_prompt, text))
  out  <- query(msgs, model = model, screen = FALSE, output = "text", format = "json",
                model_params = list(temperature = 0, seed = 42))
  jsonlite::fromJSON(out)
}

# one call per patent: its cobalt context sentences joined together
ask_llm(paste(ctx$prev_sentence, ctx$sentence, ctx$next_sentence, collapse = " "))
```

Some practical lessons:

- **Ask for JSON** (`format = "json"`) and fixed answer categories, so the output can be parsed into a table.
- **Set `temperature = 0`** so the same input gives the same answer.
- **Ask for a quote, and check it.** A model can make things up. If the quote is not in the text, the answer is thrown away. An element named as a replacement counts only if it is also named in the patent:

```{r}
#| label: llm-check
#| eval: false
ans$quote_found <- stri_detect_fixed(patent_text, ans$quote)
ans$in_text     <- vapply(ans$replacement, function(el) stri_detect_regex(patent_text, el), logical(1))
```

- **Validate by hand.** Read a random sample of answers before using them. In this project, a first check of 14 replacement answers found about a quarter doubtful (for example nitrogen named as a replacement for cobalt).

# Finding themes with a topic model {#sec-topics}

Cue words and LLM questions only find what we look for. A **topic model** works the other way round: it groups patents that are about similar things and tells us what each group is about. For cobalt, the largest themes include "cobalt-based turbine component coatings", "lithium cobalt oxide battery materials", "cemented carbide cutting tools", "Fischer-Tropsch hydrocarbon synthesis" and even "endovascular valve prostheses": finer than any category we would set in advance.

We used **BERTopic**, which works in four steps:

1. **Embed**: each title and abstract becomes a vector of numbers, using a language model trained on patents ([BERT for Patents](https://huggingface.co/anferico/bert-for-patents)). Texts about similar things get similar vectors.
2. **Reduce**: UMAP squeezes the 1,024 dimensions to a handful, keeping close texts close.
3. **Cluster**: HDBSCAN finds dense groups of texts. Each group is a topic. Texts that fit no group are assigned to the nearest one.
4. **Describe**: class-based TF-IDF finds the words that are typical for each topic, and a local LLM writes a short label from those words and three typical abstracts.

The model was run separately for 47 materials (about 131,000 patents with a strong mention and an English abstract), with [Rhobots](https://github.com/JPvdP/RhoBots), an R package that runs the whole BERTopic pipeline in R:

```{r}
#| label: bertopic
#| eval: false
library(Rhobots)

enc  <- load_hf_bert("anferico/bert-for-patents")
docs <- paste(pat$title, pat$abstract, sep = ". ")        # patents strongly naming one material
emb  <- embed_texts_cached(enc, docs, cache_file = "emb_cobalt.rds", normalize = TRUE)

fit <- fit_bertopic(docs, embeddings = emb,
                    umap_n_neighbors  = 15L,
                    umap_n_components = 5L,
                    hdbscan_min_pts   = max(10L, length(docs) %/% 300L),   # grows with the corpus
                    ngram_range       = c(1L, 2L),
                    reduce_frequent_words = TRUE,
                    seed = 42L)
fit <- reduce_outliers(fit, strategy = "embeddings", threshold = 0)       # no "noise" topic
fit <- label_topics_llm(fit, provider = "ollama", model = "mistral-small3.2",
                        top_n_terms = 10L, n_representative_docs = 3L)
```

Once each patent has a topic, the topic can be joined to the inventors' regions and the applicants. That is what the topic map in @sec-results shows: per theme, where it is invented and by whom, and whether it grew between 2010–15 and 2016–21.

::: {.callout-note}
## Dictionary or topic model?

The two approaches answer different questions. A **dictionary** answers "where is X mentioned?" with high precision, if you know what to look for. A **topic model** answers "what is in here?" without knowing in advance. The topics depend on choices (the embedding model, the minimum topic size), and their labels are written by a model, so read the typical patents of each topic before you trust its label.
:::

# Wrapping up {#sec-wrapup}

From 60 GB of patent text to maps of knowledge and supply, the pipeline went through these steps:

1. **Dictionary**: names in three languages, symbols and formulas, with domain knowledge about false friends.
2. **Clean and split**: remove markup without breaking formulas, and split sentences around abbreviations.
3. **Match and keep context**: regular expressions with lookarounds; the sentence before and after each mention.
4. **Check precision**: read samples, write rules, measure again; score relevance.
5. **Classify uses**: cue words for broad uses, an LLM for open questions, always with a check.
6. **Find themes**: a topic model on the patents that are really about a material.
7. **Link** to places: inventor regions, applicants and deposits.

The most important lesson is not technical: **look at your matches**. Every count on this page changed after we read samples of what was found.
